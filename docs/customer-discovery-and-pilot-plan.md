# Customer Discovery and Pilot Plan

_Last updated: 2026-04-24_

## 1. Purpose

The next business milestone is not just building the app. It is proving that the right customers care enough to use it, pay for it, or sponsor it.

Voice Match / YTIM should validate three connected questions:

1. Do high-value attendees feel the pain strongly enough?
2. Do organizers see measurable event value?
3. Do sponsors see better ROI than badge scans, business cards, and generic lead lists?

Think of the pilot like a small bridge test before building the highway. We do not need every feature. We need enough structure to prove people will cross.

## 2. Discovery Targets

### Interview Cluster A: Event Organizers

Target: 8–12 interviews.

Who:

- Conference organizers
- Association event managers
- Private donor-event hosts
- Executive retreat planners
- Church / nonprofit gala planners
- Startup ecosystem event operators

Questions to answer:

- What makes an event successful?
- What sponsor metrics are currently promised?
- What is hard to measure today?
- What data would change event decisions?
- What privacy lines cannot be crossed?
- What would make a pilot easy to approve?

### Interview Cluster B: Sponsors and Exhibitors

Target: 6–10 interviews.

Who:

- Corporate sponsor leads
- Booth teams
- Business development leaders
- University advancement / donor engagement teams
- Vendor marketing teams

Questions to answer:

- How do sponsors define ROI?
- What makes a lead worth following up?
- What is wrong with badge scans?
- What would make a sponsor pay extra?
- What data is useful versus creepy?

### Interview Cluster C: High-Value Attendees

Target: 10–15 interviews.

Who:

- Executives
- Sales leaders
- Founders
- Fundraisers
- Board members
- Political/community leaders
- People who attend many networking events

Questions to answer:

- When do they forget names?
- What do they wish they remembered?
- Would they opt into voice-based matching?
- What would make them trust the product?
- Would they use it before, during, or after events?

### Interview Cluster D: Privacy / Legal / Security Advisors

Target: 3–5 interviews.

Who:

- Privacy counsel
- Security leaders
- Event-risk managers
- Biometric/privacy experts

Questions to answer:

- What is the safest way to pilot?
- What consent language is needed?
- What should not be collected?
- What deletion/retention policy is expected?

## 3. Customer Discovery Script

### Opening

"We are exploring a permissioned event relationship assistant that helps attendees remember who they meet, helps organizers understand engagement, and helps sponsors improve follow-up. We are not selling yet. We are trying to learn where the pain is real."

### Attendee Questions

1. Tell me about the last event where you met many people.
2. What was hard about remembering names or follow-up details?
3. What did you do after the event to follow up?
4. What tools did you use?
5. Where did those tools fail?
6. Would a permissioned app that helped you remember people be useful?
7. What would make it feel helpful rather than creepy?
8. Would voice recognition be acceptable if everyone explicitly opted in?
9. What would you pay for personally, if anything?
10. What would your organization pay for?

### Organizer Questions

1. How do you measure event success today?
2. How do you prove value to sponsors?
3. What engagement data do you wish you had?
4. What are your current tools?
5. What parts of event networking are still manual?
6. What would a successful pilot need to show?
7. Who would need to approve a pilot?
8. What privacy concerns would stop this immediately?
9. What would you pay for if this worked?
10. What would make this a must-have rather than a nice-to-have?

### Sponsor Questions

1. How do you measure sponsorship ROI today?
2. What makes a lead high quality?
3. What is wrong with your current lead capture process?
4. How do you follow up after an event?
5. What data would improve conversion?
6. Would aggregate booth engagement be valuable?
7. Would permissioned introductions be valuable?
8. What would be unacceptable from a privacy perspective?
9. What would you pay for better sponsor intelligence?
10. What would make you ask an organizer to include this product?

## 4. Hypotheses to Validate

| Hypothesis | Evidence Needed | Kill Criteria |
|---|---|---|
| Attendees forget names/details at high-value events | Repeated firsthand stories | Most attendees say existing tools are good enough |
| Organizers need better engagement data | Organizer requests for real-time/post-event insights | Organizers only care about attendance counts |
| Sponsors dislike badge-scan quality | Sponsors report noisy follow-up lists | Sponsors are satisfied with current CRM flows |
| Consent-first voice matching is acceptable | Users accept opt-in framing | Users reject voice matching even with consent |
| Small curated pilots are viable | Organizer agrees to pilot path | Approval/privacy concerns block every pilot |
| Sponsor analytics can monetize | Sponsors show willingness to pay | Sponsors view it as organizer-only value |

## 5. Pilot Event Design

### Ideal Pilot

- 20–100 people.
- Curated group with strong networking need.
- Clear organizer relationship.
- Low legal complexity.
- Participants can opt in before the event.
- Sponsor or organizer wants a post-event engagement report.

### Good Pilot Types

- Executive roundtable.
- Founder/investor mixer.
- Board/donor reception.
- Association networking event.
- Sponsor-hosted VIP event.
- Professional-services client event.

### Bad First Pilots

- Large anonymous conferences.
- Events with minors.
- Events involving highly sensitive political, health, or religious identity data.
- Events where participants cannot meaningfully consent.
- Events where hidden tracking is expected.

## 6. Pilot MVP Flow

### Before Event

1. Organizer creates event.
2. Attendees are imported or invited.
3. Participants see consent explanation.
4. Participants opt in.
5. Participants create profile and voice enrollment.

### During Event

1. Attendee can identify opted-in participants in controlled flow.
2. Attendee can add notes.
3. Attendee can correct wrong matches.
4. Organizer can see aggregate opt-in and engagement counts.

### After Event

1. Attendee receives personal follow-up memory list.
2. Organizer receives aggregate report.
3. Sponsor receives permissioned/aggregate sponsor report.
4. Team collects feedback and objections.

## 7. Pilot Success Metrics

### Product Metrics

- Opt-in rate.
- Enrollment completion rate.
- Recognition success rate in controlled conditions.
- Manual correction rate.
- Follow-up-list usage.
- Deletion/opt-out rate.

### Business Metrics

- Organizer satisfaction.
- Sponsor satisfaction.
- Willingness to pay.
- Referral likelihood.
- Repeat-event interest.
- Number of post-pilot sales conversations.

### Trust Metrics

- Users understood the consent model.
- Users did not feel secretly surveilled.
- Users knew how to opt out.
- No participant data was exposed beyond permissions.

## 10. Decision Gate

After 25–30 interviews, decide:

1. Is event networking the right wedge?
2. Is voice recognition a must-have, nice-to-have, or risky distraction?
3. Is the buyer organizer, sponsor, attendee, or a bundled model?
4. Which event type is best for the first pilot?
5. What feature can be removed without hurting pilot proof?

## 11. Working Rule

Do not confuse applause with evidence.

Evidence is when someone agrees to introduce you, pilot it, pay for it, share data, or change their workflow.
